About the deep

The engine: reefs, currents, and vents.

These three systems—coral reefs, the global conveyor belt, and hydrothermal vents—define how the ocean stores heat, supports life, and redistributes energy.

Coral Reefs

Covering less than 1% of the seafloor, coral reefs support roughly 25% of marine species. They are biodiversity hotspots and coastal defenders.

Global Conveyor Belt

A hidden river of deep currents moves heat and salt around the globe, regulating climate on planetary scales.

Hydrothermal Vents

Alien ecosystems rely on toxic chemistry, thriving where sunlight never reaches and temperatures can exceed 400°C.

Conveyor Impact

By moving heat and nutrients vertically and across basins, deep currents knit local ecosystems into global climate responses.

Mysteries

The deep still holds most of the unknown.

Layered currents and bioluminescent zones are explained in concise cards so the science reads clearly at a glance.

Below the waves, scale and pressure invent new rules. These features explore myth and measurement—where story meets the smallest scientific instrument.

The Mariana Trench

Thirty-six thousand feet down, the Challenger Deep is a cathedral of pressure—every square inch carries the weight of eight tons above. In that silence, life adapts at physical limits, and the instruments we send become small, fragile emissaries.

The Kraken

What once was myth took shape in the Giant Squid: enormous, elusive, and warlike in the stories sailors tell. These deep cephalopods wage silent wars in black water—an evolutionary theatre where strength, disguise, and sensory cunning decide survival.

The Alien World Below

We have mapped Mars better than our seafloor. Eighty percent of the ocean remains unexplored—an alien continent of vents, trenches, and biological oddities that defy surface assumptions about life.

Four layers, one descent.

01
Depth: 0–200m
Light: Sunlight
Feature: Photosynthesis

Epipelagic

0 - 200m. Also known as the sunlight zone, it receives enough sunlight for photosynthesis to occur. As a result, it is home to most of the ocean's plant life, including phytoplankton, which forms the base of the marine food web.

The abundance of food and light supports a massive diversity of life, ranging from tiny zooplankton to massive apex predators like great white sharks, dolphins, and tuna. The water temperature is relatively warm and strongly influenced by the sun and surface winds, leading to well-mixed layers.

Because visibility is high, many species here rely on sight for hunting and evasion, featuring countershading as a primary camouflage mechanism. It acts as the primary heat engine and oxygen producer for the entire planet.

02
Depth: 200–1,000m
Light: Twilight
Feature: Bioluminescence

Mesopelagic

200 - 1,000m. Often referred to as the twilight zone, this layer receives only faint, filtered traces of sunlight—insufficient for photosynthesis. Consequently, no plants can grow here, and food becomes increasingly scarce.

Animals in this zone have evolved incredible adaptations to survive, with many developing large, upward-looking eyes to catch the faintest silhouettes of prey against the surface. Bioluminescence is highly common, with fish, squid, and jellyfish using specialized light-producing organs to communicate, attract prey, or camouflage themselves.

03
Depth: 1,000–4,000m
Light: Midnight
Feature: Low-energy adaptations

Bathypelagic

1,000 - 4,000m. Known as the midnight zone, this layer exists in perpetual, crushing darkness. The only light visible here is the occasional blue-green flash of bioluminescence produced by the bizarre creatures that call this extreme environment home.

The water pressure is immense, reaching nearly 6,000 pounds per square inch, and temperatures hover just barely above freezing. Because food is incredibly scarce, animals here survive on "marine snow"—a constant drift of organic detritus falling from the upper layers.

04
Depth: 4,000–6,000m
Light: Pitch black
Feature: Whale-fall ecosystems

Abyssopelagic

4,000 - 6,000m. The lower abyss is a desolate, freezing, and nearly pitch-black realm that covers the vast majority of the ocean floor. The name "abyss" originates from a Greek word meaning "no bottom," perfectly describing this seemingly endless expanse of mud and fine sediment.

The crushing hydrostatic pressure here is so intense that it would instantly collapse human lungs, yet a surprising array of specialized life thrives. The ecosystem relies entirely on dead biological matter settling from above, including massive whale falls that can sustain localized communities for decades.
